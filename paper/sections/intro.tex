\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The efficiency argument for spiking neural networks (SNNs) rests on an unverified
premise: that they fire sparsely. Models trained for accuracy alone are rarely audited
for how much they actually fire. Under a standard direct-training setup --- ResNet-19 on
CIFAR-10, $T=4$ --- training with no spike regularisation converges to 488,619 spikes
(n=4) \cite{wu2018spatio,neftci2019surrogate,zheng2021going}.

Half of that firing turns out to be unnecessary. Under the same setup, cutting spikes by
51.7\% leaves accuracy unchanged: the unregularised baseline reaches
$96.720 \pm 0.067$ at 488,619 spikes (n=4), while our regulariser at
$\rho{=}3{\times}10^{-3}$ reaches $96.650 \pm 0.168$ at 235,802 spikes (n=4), a 51.7\%
reduction. The gap between the two, $-0.070$ percentage points, is about one baseline
standard deviation, and it sits above the lossless line (baseline mean minus two
standard deviations, 96.585). Directly trained SNNs therefore converge to an
over-firing state.

Regularisers that penalise the total spike count date back to 2016, and two problems
have persisted since. First, their strength has to be hand-tuned: Sorbaro et al.\ fix
their coefficient at $\alpha=1/S_0^2$, a constant set before training begins, and
instead sweep the absolute SynOps target $S_0$ by hand across 30 models
\cite{sorbaro2020optimizing}. Activity Pruning (NeurIPS 2025) hardcodes its strength
coefficients into per-dataset configuration files; moving from its CIFAR-10 setting
($10^{-3}$/$10^{-4}$) to its ImageNet-100 setting ($10^{-4}$/$10^{-2}$) moves one
coefficient $100\times$ and the other $10\times$, in opposite directions
\cite{bu2025activity}. Both cases show the same failure: strength must be re-tuned by
hand for every dataset and every target. Second, reporting stays at the level of
simulated proxies --- Sorbaro et al.\ come from the group that manufactures the Speck
sensor, yet report only sinabs simulation results, never a hardware measurement
\cite{neil2016learning,sorbaro2020optimizing,pellegrini2021low,meda2022improving}.

Curves are not the whole story. In our own exploration of per-neuron weightings inside
the spike-count penalty, changing the weighting rule moved the operating point along the
same accuracy-spike curve rather than off it (observed on VGG-16/CIFAR-10; not reported
here) --- but the same point on that curve does not mean the same internal state. The observation that
spike reduction is mostly neuron silencing was made first by Activity Pruning
(NeurIPS 2025) \cite{bu2025activity}; what we add is that \emph{which} neurons are
silenced differs by method. Regressing log active-neuron count on log spike count and
method (coefficient 0.869, $t=-21.5$, $n=21$) shows that at matched spike budgets, our
regulariser uses 13.1\% fewer active neurons. The nearest spike-matched pair makes the
same point directly, though it is a separate, single-pair measurement and not what the
regression explains: at 193,129 spikes our regulariser uses 131,641 neurons, against
193,083 spikes and 155,669 neurons for the layer-wise $L_2$ penalty --- a 15.4\%
difference in neuron count at an almost identical spike count. Per-neuron firing rate
confirms the mechanism is not uniform: 1.621 for the unregularised baseline, 1.479 for
our regulariser, 1.370 for the softmax weighting, and 1.284 for the layer-wise $L_2$
penalty (ours vs.\ the $L_2$ penalty: $t=10.5$, $p=8.4{\times}10^{-7}$). Spike reduction
is not one mechanism: the layer-wise $L_2$ penalty weakens every neuron a little, while
our regulariser silences some neurons outright.

So what does using fewer neurons change in practice? We answer this not with the
fraction of neurons quiet on a given image, but with the fraction of neurons that never
fire across the entire test set. Over all 10,000 test images, the permanently silent
fraction is __G5_SILENT_RATIO__.\footnote{Measured with
\texttt{extract\_neuron\_spikes.py --split test --n 10000}.} The silence fractions
reported elsewhere in this paper are measured on 100-image batches and cannot be
read as ``permanently dead neurons'' --- conflating the two would overstate the
pruning benefit.

Taken together, this paper makes the following contributions.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item At a matched spike budget, our regulariser uses 13.1\% fewer active neurons than
  the layer-wise $L_2$ penalty (ANCOVA coefficient 0.869, $t=-21.5$, $n=21$,
  ResNet-19/CIFAR-10): the same point on the accuracy-spike curve does not imply the
  same internal state.
  % GATE: G4
  \item We set the regularisation strength through a single dimensionless ratio between
  the task loss and the regularisation term, needing neither an absolute SynOps target
  nor prior knowledge of the spike budget. This tentatively distinguishes our approach
  from Sorbaro et al.'s static $1/S_0^2$ and Activity Pruning's hand-set coefficients,
  pending a control that applies the same ratio controller to the layer-wise $L_2$
  penalty.
\end{itemize}
